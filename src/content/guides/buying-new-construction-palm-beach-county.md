New construction is a big part of the housing market in Palm Beach County, especially out west — west Boca Raton, west Delray Beach, Boynton Beach, Lake Worth, and the growing communities toward Wellington. A brand-new home has obvious appeal: nobody has lived in it, the roof and systems are new, and you pick the finishes. But buying from a builder is a different process from buying a resale home, and it helps to know where it differs before you walk into a sales center.

### The sales center works for the builder

Start with the most important fact: the friendly person in the model home represents the builder, not you. Their job is to sell that builder's homes at that builder's prices and terms. Nothing about that is dishonest — it's just the arrangement, and you should walk in understanding it.

That's the main reason to bring your own agent. Builder policies on working with a buyer's agent vary, and those policies have been changing — don't assume representation is automatic or at no cost. Ask how it works at that community before you go. There's also a catch worth knowing: many builders require your agent to be registered with you on your first visit. If you tour a community on your own and sign in without your agent, the builder may refuse to work with them later. So if you think you might want an agent on a new-construction purchase, bring them from the start, or at least call before your first visit.

### The price on the sign is the starting point

The advertised price is the base price of the home — the structure on a standard lot, with standard finishes. Almost nobody pays the base price, because almost nobody buys the standard everything. Lot premiums apply to the better lots: lake views, preserve views, corner lots, larger lots. Then come the options — flooring, cabinets, countertops, lighting, a summer kitchen, a pool. The model home you're walking through is usually loaded with upgrades, and the gap between the base price and the model-home price can be substantial.

That doesn't make new construction a bad deal. It means you should price the home you actually want, with the lot and the options you actually care about, and compare that full number against resale homes. Spend your upgrade money on the things that are hard to change later — the structure, the layout, the lot — and be careful with expensive finishes you could do yourself for less after you move in.

### Deposits and timelines

Builders take deposits in stages rather than all at once: money down when you sign the contract, often more when you make your design selections, with the balance at closing. The deposit structure is set out in the builder's contract, and it's generally less flexible than a resale contract. Read the deposit terms carefully, especially what happens to your money if the deal doesn't close.

Timelines deserve the same care. A home that hasn't been started yet can take many months to build, and completion dates in builder contracts are usually estimates with room built in for the builder. Weather, permits, and materials can all push a closing back. If you have a hard deadline — a lease ending, a home up north to sell, a school year starting — talk about it plainly before you sign, and keep a cushion in your plans. Some buyers solve this by choosing a home that's already under construction or a completed inventory home, which trades some choice for a much shorter wait.

### The builder's contract is the builder's contract

Resale purchases here usually run on standard Florida real estate contracts. Builders use their own contracts, written by their own lawyers. Terms on deposits, delays, warranties, and what happens if something goes wrong will differ from what you'd see in a resale deal, and builders rarely agree to many changes. That makes it even more important to read the whole thing — or have your own agent and, where it's warranted, your own attorney read it — before you sign, not after.

On the plus side, new homes come with builder warranties, and Florida law provides certain warranty protections on new construction. Know what your warranty covers and for how long, and keep the builder's warranty contact information somewhere you'll find it in month eleven, not just moving week.

### Inspections still matter

A new home is not automatically a perfect home. You can and should have your own independent inspection done before closing, even on a brand-new build. Inspectors find things in new construction regularly — small things, mostly, but occasionally things you'd want the builder to fix while fixing them is still the builder's problem. Some buyers inspect twice: once before the drywall goes up, if the timing allows, and once before closing.

### The short version

New construction can be a great buy in Palm Beach County — new systems, your choices, builder warranty, and communities with amenities that resale neighborhoods can't match. Just go in knowing the base price isn't the price, the timeline is the builder's, the contract is the builder's too, and the sales center isn't on your side. Your own agent, registered on your first visit, changes that balance.
