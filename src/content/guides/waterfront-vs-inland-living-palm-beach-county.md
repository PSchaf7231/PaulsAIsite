Almost everybody who moves to Palm Beach County asks some version of the same question: is it worth paying for the water? There's no single right answer — there are people who would never live anywhere but on the Intracoastal, and people who tried the beach area, moved west, and never looked back. What I can do is lay out the real trade-offs, the way they actually play out in daily life and in your monthly bills, so you can decide which side of the trade you're on.

### What "waterfront" means here

Waterfront isn't one thing in this county. At the top there's direct oceanfront — condos and homes on the sand in places like Highland Beach, Delray Beach, and Boca Raton, where your view is the Atlantic itself. Then there's the Intracoastal Waterway and the canals off it, where homes come with docks and boat lifts and you can be out to the ocean through an inlet. There are the wide lakes in the western communities — not the ocean, but real water views, sunsets over the lake, and a waterfront feel at an inland price. And there are beach-area homes that aren't on the water at all but are east of the Intracoastal, a short walk from the sand. Each of those is a different product, a different price, and a different life.

### The case for the water

The view is not a small thing. People underestimate it before they live with it — the light, the boats going by, the breeze, the fact that the best part of your home is the part you didn't have to furnish. If you own a boat, dockage behind your own house changes boating from an expedition into an errand; people who have it use their boats far more. Beach access shapes your days in a way that's hard to price: morning walks on the sand become a habit, not a plan.

There's also a plain market fact: there is only so much waterfront, and they're not making more of it. Waterfront property in established east-side locations has historically been the most supply-limited segment of this market. That doesn't guarantee anything about any particular home's future value, and I won't promise you appreciation — but the scarcity itself is real, and it's part of what you're paying for.

### The costs of the water

Now the other side, because waterfront living bills you for the view in several currencies.

**Price.** Waterfront carries a premium — in many cases a large one — over a comparable home inland. You are paying for location and scarcity more than for the house itself, and two homes with the same square footage can be far apart in price based on which side of the Intracoastal they sit.

**Insurance.** Homes near the water generally cost more to insure. Wind exposure is the obvious reason, and flood insurance enters the picture for many waterfront and low-lying properties — sometimes required by a lender, sometimes simply wise. On a waterfront purchase, insurance quotes belong at the front of the process, not the back.

**Maintenance.** Salt air is hard on a house. It corrodes metal, ages air conditioning equipment and outdoor fixtures faster, and shortens the life of anything that lives outside. Docks, seawalls, and lifts are their own category of upkeep — a seawall is a major structure with a lifespan, and its condition is a real item to inspect before you buy, because replacing one is a serious expense. None of this should scare you off. All of it should be in your budget.

**The seasonal crowd.** The beach areas are where the winter visitors are. In season, the restaurants are full, the roads near the beach are slow, and the quiet street you saw in September is a different place in February. Some people love the energy. Some people move inland partly to escape it.

### The case for inland

Inland — meaning the communities west of the Intracoastal, and especially the newer ones out west — buys you more house. Newer construction, more square footage, modern layouts, and community amenities like clubhouses, pools, tennis, and fitness centers, generally at a lower price per square foot than the east side. Many inland communities are gated and maintained to a high standard, and for families, the west side often lines up better with the schools and daily routines they care about.

The maintenance picture is gentler too: no salt air chewing on your equipment, no seawall, and insurance that usually comes in lower than a comparable home on the water. And plenty of inland homes sit on lakes, so "inland" doesn't have to mean giving up a water view — it can mean a lake view without oceanfront costs.

What you give up is the ocean itself and the east side's character — the older neighborhoods, the downtowns of Delray Beach and Boca Raton close by, and the ability to walk to the beach. For some people that trade is unthinkable. For others, it's the smartest money they ever spent. Both are right.

### How to decide

Ask yourself how you'll actually live. If you'll boat every week, a dock is worth more than a fourth bedroom. If the beach is a twice-a-year event for you, paying the waterfront premium to be near it is buying a view you won't use. Think about the monthly bills, not just the purchase price — insurance and maintenance differences follow you every year you own the home. See [what it costs to live in Palm Beach County](/guides/what-it-costs-to-live-in-palm-beach-county) for that full picture. And if you're torn, do what I tell relocation clients to do: rent on the side you're leaning toward for a stretch, and let a season of real life settle it.

There is no wrong answer here — there's only the answer that fits your life and your budget. My job is to make sure you see both sides with real numbers before you choose.
