The purchase price is the number everybody watches. It is not the number that decides whether a home actually fits your life. Down here, the monthly cost of owning a house in Palm Beach County is made of several pieces, and a couple of them surprise people moving from other states. This page walks through the real ones, so you can run an honest number before you fall in love with a kitchen.

### Property taxes

Florida property taxes are paid to the county, and Palm Beach County taxes are based on the assessed value of your home. The bill you see on a listing is the current owner's bill, not necessarily yours. When a home changes hands, the assessment can reset closer to the sale price, which means your tax bill can be higher than the one the seller has been paying. Budget for your own number, not theirs.

One thing that works in your favor: taxes are billed once a year, and the county offers discounts for paying early. It's a small thing, but on a Florida-sized tax bill, small percentages are real money.

### The homestead exemption

If the home is your primary residence, Florida's homestead exemption is one of the best deals in state law. Filing for it takes a portion of your home's assessed value off the tax roll, which lowers your bill. Just as important, homestead status limits how much your assessed value can rise each year, which helps keep the tax bill more predictable. Tax rates and other charges can still change, and if you already have a Florida homestead, ask about portability before you buy.

Two things people get wrong about it. First, it is not automatic — you have to apply for it with the Palm Beach County Property Appraiser, and there is a filing deadline each year. Confirm the current date with the appraiser's office when you buy. Second, it only applies to the home you actually live in as your primary residence. A second home or an investment property doesn't get it. When you buy, put the homestead filing on your calendar the same week you get your keys.

### Homeowners insurance — the Florida reality

This is the line item that shocks people. Florida homeowners insurance costs more than in most of the country, and it isn't close. Storms are the reason, and insurers price for the risk of a bad season, not an average one.

What actually drives your premium is the house itself: the age and condition of the roof, how the home was built, whether the windows and doors are impact-rated or have shutters, and how close you are to the water. Two houses on the same street can carry very different premiums. A newer roof and impact glass can make a real difference, which is why I tell buyers to ask about insurance while they're still shopping — not after they're under contract and it's too late to do anything about it.

A few practical points. Get insurance quotes early in the buying process, because an older home with an older roof can be hard or expensive to insure, and that can change whether a deal works at all. Expect a separate, higher deductible for hurricane damage — that's standard in Florida policies. And if a lender is involved, insurance isn't optional, so treat it as part of the price of the house, not an afterthought.

### Association fees

A large share of homes in Boca Raton, Delray Beach, and the surrounding towns sit inside communities with a homeowners association or a condo association. The fees vary widely — a modest neighborhood association might cover little more than common-area landscaping, while a full-service community with a clubhouse, security gate, and amenities costs considerably more. Condos tend to carry heavier fees because the association insures and maintains the building itself.

Read the fee as part of the purchase, not a footnote. A cheaper house with heavy monthly fees can cost more over a year than a pricier house with light ones. Also ask what the fee covers, whether there are any special assessments coming, and whether the association requires an application and approval before you can buy. Many do, and that adds time to your closing.

### The other regular costs

Beyond the big three, plan on the ordinary Florida extras: higher electric bills in the summer months when the air conditioning runs around the clock, lawn and pool service if the house has them (many associations require you to maintain them), and flood insurance if the property sits in a flood zone and a lender requires it — or if you simply want the protection. None of these are deal-breakers. All of them belong in the monthly number you run before you make an offer.

### The short version

Run the full picture: mortgage, taxes at your likely assessment, insurance quoted on that actual house, association fees, and utilities. If the home still fits comfortably with all of it on the page, it's a good buy. If it only works when you leave pieces out, it doesn't work.
