Renting here is not like renting in most places, and the people who get burned are usually the ones who assume it works the way it did back home. Here's how it actually works in Boca Raton and Delray Beach, so you know what you're looking at before you fall for a listing.

### Seasonal vs. annual — the first thing to understand

Almost every rental here is one of two kinds. A **seasonal rental** runs for a few months, usually covering the winter, and it's almost always furnished — furniture, kitchen gear, linens, the works. You bring a suitcase. A seasonal rents for a much higher monthly price than the same home on an annual lease, because you're paying for the best months of the year and for everything in it.

An **annual rental** is a year or longer, usually unfurnished, and it's for people who are actually living here year-round — working here, settling in, or testing the area before buying. When you see a rental price online, the first question is always: is that the [seasonal](/guides/seasonal-rentals-palm-beach-county) price or the annual price? The same house can have both, and mixing them up is the most common misunderstanding in this market.

Some homes offer both, and some landlords will consider a shorter term at the right price. It never hurts to ask — but ask, don't assume.

### Furnished means different things to different people

A furnished seasonal usually means you can walk in with a bag and live there. An annual rental described as furnished is rarer and worth a closer look — sometimes it means the owner left the furniture because it was easier than moving it. Either way, if furniture matters to you, ask for current photos of what's actually in the home, not the photos from when it was staged for sale three years ago.

### What landlords and associations ask for

Expect an application, a credit and background check, and proof of income. That's standard. What surprises people is the second layer: if the home is in a community with a homeowners association or a condo association, the association often has its own application, its own fee, and its own approval — and its own timeline. An association approval can take a couple of weeks, and the lease can't start until it comes through. If you need to move on a specific date, that approval clock matters as much as the landlord's answer.

Associations also have rental rules. Many communities limit how often a home can be rented, require a minimum lease length, or cap the number of rentals in the community at one time. A condo near the beach may not allow a three-month rental at all. This is why two similar-looking homes can have completely different answers to the same request.

### How fast good ones go — and why so many listings are already gone

A fairly priced rental in a good location moves fast, especially heading into season. Days, not weeks. And here's the part that frustrates everybody: a lot of the rentals you see posted online are already rented. Listings get copied from site to site, old posts never come down, and a home that rented a week ago can keep collecting inquiries for a month. I hear "I called five people and every place was gone" from almost every renter I work with. It's not you. It's the listings.

That's the whole reason to work from the MLS instead of the open internet. The MLS is where the listing actually lives — when a home rents, its status changes there. When I send a renter homes, they're homes that are on the MLS and still available that day. It saves you the week of phone calls to places that don't exist anymore.

### What to do before you start

Get your paperwork together first: ID, proof of income, and be ready for the credit check. Decide your real move-in date and whether you can bend it by a couple of weeks for an association approval. Know your must-haves versus your nice-to-haves — a fenced yard for a dog will cut your choices in half, and it's better to know that on day one than day twenty. And if you have a pet, say so up front. Plenty of landlords take pets; plenty don't; finding out at the application stage wastes everybody's time.
